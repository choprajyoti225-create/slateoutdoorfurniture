How much parasol base weight you actually need

The number on the box assumes still air. Real figures by canopy size, plus why a half-open parasol is the dangerous one.

Time
Ten minutes
Best season
Summer
Reading
2 min
Written
2 May 2026

A parasol is a sail. The force on it rises with the square of wind speed, so a breeze that doubles from 10 to 20mph does not double the load — it quadruples it. This is why bases sold as adequate are routinely not.

Working figures

For a centre-pole parasol on a free-standing base, with no table helping:

Canopy diameter Minimum base Comfortable
2.0m 15kg 20kg
2.5m 20kg 30kg
3.0m 30kg 40kg
3.5m+ 45kg 60kg+

If the pole passes through a table's centre hole and the table is loaded, you can drop to roughly the "minimum" column — the table is doing the stabilising and the base is mostly holding the pole vertical. An empty lightweight table does not count; it will go over with the parasol.

Cantilever parasols are a different calculation entirely. The offset mast means the base resists a turning moment, not just a push, and they want 60–90kg for a 3m canopy. Most are sold with a cross-base designed to take four paving slabs. Use four slabs. All four.

Fill it with sand, not water

Hollow fillable bases take either. Dry sand is about 1.6 times denser than water, so the same base holds appreciably more mass — and it cannot freeze and split the moulding in January. Damp builder's sand is heavier still and packs tighter. The only argument for water is that you can empty it easily.

The half-open trap

A parasol left partly open in wind is far more dangerous than one fully open, because the canopy becomes a scoop that catches gusts and cannot spill them. Fully open, the fabric flexes and vents; half open, it loads one side and levers the pole.

Closed and tied is the only safe way to leave one standing. Untied, the canopy works loose in the night and you have a half-open parasol.

Rules of thumb worth keeping

  • If you can rock the base with one hand, wind can move it.
  • Take the canopy down before you leave the house, not before the forecast rain.
  • A parasol that has blown over once has usually bent its ribs slightly; it will fold more easily the second time. Check the rib joints after any fall.

You will need

  • Tape measure
