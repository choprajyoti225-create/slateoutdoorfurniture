Who writes this

Written at a bench,not at a desk

I am Ines Brandwood. I restore and repair outdoor furniture from a workshop in Totnes, Devon — mostly teak that has been left out for fifteen years, and mostly for people who were about to throw it away.

The same eight or nine problems arrive on a trailer every spring. A chair that racks diagonally because it was bolted up in mid-air. A leg rusted from the inside out because a plastic foot fell off in 2019. A cushion set that has been cleaned four times and still smells, because nobody ever took the foam out.

None of these are difficult. They are just invisible until someone points at them. So this site is the pointing — the 12 things I end up explaining most often, written down properly so I can send a link instead of a paragraph.

What it is not

It is not a shop, and it is not a review site. There is no "best garden furniture 2026" list here, because the honest answer to that question is almost always "the second-hand teak set eleven miles away, for a third of the money", and that does not make for a good listicle.

How the advice is chosen

Nothing is recommended that has not been done
Every method here has been used on a real set, usually more than once. Where something is a rule of thumb rather than a measured figure, it says so.
The cheap fix comes first
A bag of plastic feet costs less than a pound and prevents more damage than any cover. Where a £4 answer and a £400 answer both work, the £4 one leads.
No affiliate links, no product rankings
Specific brands are named only where they are genuinely useful — mostly on the second-hand market, where parts availability years later is the whole argument. Nothing here is sponsored.
Saying "leave it alone" is an answer
Silvered teak, surface checking, black spotting on old oil: a lot of what looks like damage is cosmetic and reversible. Knowing what to ignore saves more furniture than any technique.
